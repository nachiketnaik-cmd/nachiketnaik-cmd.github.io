Indian investors buy the dip

What 36 months of mutual fund data say about how we react when the market falls

There's a common story about retail investors: they pile in when markets are rising and panic when they fall. It's called return chasing, and it's meant to explain why ordinary investors so often buy high and sell low.

I wanted to check whether that story holds for Indian mutual fund investors, so I collected three years of data and tested it. The answer surprised me. Indian investors seem to do the opposite.

The data

Every month, AMFI (the industry body for Indian mutual funds) publishes how much money flowed into and out of each type of fund. I downloaded 36 of these reports, from October 2023 to September 2026, and matched them with the Nifty 50's return for each month. Then I asked a simple question: when the market has a bad month, do equity funds get less money, or more?

What I found

More. In the 17 months when the Nifty fell, equity funds took in an average of Rs 32,329 crore. In the 19 months when it rose, they took in Rs 27,390 crore.

A regression puts a number on it. Each 1% fall in the Nifty went with about Rs 733 crore more flowing into equity funds that same month. For a 5% fall, that's roughly Rs 3,700 crore extra, about 12% more than an average month.

The biggest falls make the point clearly:

Three of the four largest falls in the period came with some of the largest inflows. October 2024 was the second-biggest inflow month of all 36.

Is it real, or a fluke?

With only 36 months of data, I didn't want to trust one regression, so I ran two checks.

  • Flows are "sticky": a strong month tends to follow a strong month, which can make a result look more certain than it is. Adding last month's flow to the model didn't change the answer, and the effect stayed significant.
  • One unusual month can drive everything. I re-ran the regression 36 times, leaving out a different month each time. The effect was negative every single time.

I also checked timing. The reaction happens in the same month as the fall. Last month's return adds very little once you know this month's. Investors aren't waiting to see what happens; they react quickly.

How much of the money comes through SIPs?

A large and growing share of the money entering equity funds arrives through SIPs: fixed amounts invested every month, whatever the market is doing.

SIP figures cover SIPs into all fund types, not only equity funds, so the share is an upper estimate.

SIP contributions almost doubled over the period and barely moved with the market. Roughly two of every five rupees entering equity funds now come from investors on autopilot.

The market is only one part of the story

Money moves in and out of mutual funds for many reasons, and the market's monthly return is only one of them. Market returns explain about a fifth of the month-to-month swings in equity flows. The other four-fifths come from elsewhere.

"Investors pour money in when the Nifty falls" is one possible explanation for the pattern, but it's not the only one. Other forces could be at work too:

  • Macroeconomic factors such as interest rates, inflation, salary cycles and changes in tax rules.
  • Regional and local factors. Mutual fund money is concentrated in a few places. Maharashtra alone holds about 37% of the industry's assets, Delhi 15%, and Gujarat and Karnataka about 7% each, so conditions in these states can move the national numbers. Local events matter too. A cyclone on the east coast, for example, might push families to withdraw their savings to rebuild homes, recover lost land or repair damage.

The data is also mixed. AMFI's monthly flow figures don't come only from retail investors. High-net-worth (HNI) and ultra-high-net-worth (UHNI) individuals, large funds and companies also hold a mix of equity, debt and liquid funds, and their buying and selling shows up in the same totals. But who owns what differs a lot by fund type:

Equity funds, where the buy-the-dip pattern shows up, are owned overwhelmingly by individuals. Debt and liquid funds, where companies move huge sums around quarter-ends, are mostly institutional. So the equity result mostly reflects individual investors, though the monthly figures still can't separate small retail savers from wealthy HNIs.

This study doesn't separate these effects, and monthly data can't tell me whether the extra money in a falling month is new buying or simply fewer people selling. It also shows a link, not proof of cause, and the period was mostly a rising market.

My conclusion

Even with all those caveats, one thing is clear from three years of data: Indian mutual fund investors don't chase returns these days. If anything, they lean in when the market falls. Monthly SIP contributions roughly doubled over the period and barely react to the market at all, so millions of people now invest steadily whatever the headlines say.

The old picture of the investor who buys high and sells low doesn't fit India anymore. Investors here have become smarter and more nuanced in how they invest.
